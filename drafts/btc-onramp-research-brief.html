Three findings that change the article

01

The HSBC premise doesn’t hold in Hong Kong

HSBC throttles crypto in the UK (£2,500 per transaction, £10,000 per rolling 30 days) and blocks it outright in Australia. Credit-card crypto purchases are blocked globally. But Hong Kong is HSBC’s most permissive market — HSBC HK does not block transfers to SFC-licensed platforms, has offered crypto ETF access through its HK brokerage since mid-2023, and in April 2026 received one of the HKMA’s first two stablecoin issuer licences.

The bad reputation is real, but it’s a UK and Australia story that Hong Kong readers have absorbed by osmosis. Correcting this is a genuinely useful thing the article can do. If you have first-hand accounts of HK HSBC blocks, that’s worth a line — but it’s anecdote against documented policy, so frame it that way.

02

The real bank problem is the reverse of what you’d expect

The friction isn’t which bank lets you send money out. It’s that the licensed exchanges require the first deposit to arrive from a bank account in your own name — HKD 10,000 or USD equivalent minimum at both HashKey and OSL. No third-party accounts, no joint accounts.

The gating factor is having the right kind of bank account, not choosing the right bank.

03

The mainland answer changed in February 2026

On 6 February 2026, eight PRC regulators — PBOC, NDRC, MIIT, NFRA, CSRC, SAFE and others — issued the Notice on Further Preventing and Dealing with Virtual Currency and Other Related Risks, Notice No. 42 [2026]. It extends the 2021 ban: offshore entities and individuals are now prohibited from providing virtual-currency services to persons in mainland China in any form. Previously the restriction bit at onshore employees and affiliates; now it reaches the offshore provider itself.

So the honest answer to a mainland-resident friend is no longer “use OKX with your passport.” The dividing line across your whole audience is not nationality — it is residence. A PRC passport holder residing in Hong Kong or Singapore has good, legal options. A PRC passport holder residing in the mainland does not have a clean answer, and you should not supply a workaround.

The framing

The short answer

For someone with no crypto background, the answer is a licensed centralized exchange in the jurisdiction where they legally reside. Not the biggest exchange, not the cheapest one — the one that will actually accept their ID, take their money from a local bank, and still be there next year.

Everything below is organized by residence, because residence is what determines the answer.

So where did the big names go?

The obvious reader question, and the honest answer is more interesting than “grey area.” The large offshore venues didn’t quietly ignore Hong Kong’s regime — most of them applied, then formally withdrew and exited, on the public record.

Correct the frame — and this is background, not article copy

This is not quiet non-compliance. It is a documented, orderly withdrawal conducted with the regulator. Firms weigh licensing costs against market size and sometimes decline — an ordinary commercial decision, not a scandal. The point that lands hardest for your audience

Those user counts are no longer mainland Chinese users. Since Notice No. 42 (February 2026), serving mainland persons is prohibited for offshore providers in any form. Whatever the historical China base was, it is now legally cut off — which is precisely how HashKey, a licensed Hong Kong venue, took ~75% of a market these giants walked away from.

Article angle — the neutral phrasing

Your friends’ mental model is biggest equals safest. The useful correction is gentler than an accusation: size and local licensing are two different things. A global platform can be enormous, well-run, and licensed in a dozen places while simply not being licensed where you live. The question that matters to a reader isn’t which exchange is biggest — it’s which one is licensed in their own jurisdiction.

Say it this way, not that way

Prefer “the platforms licensed in Hong Kong are these” over “the big names are unlicensed here.” Same information, reader-useful, and it doesn’t read as a charge against anyone. Name where a firm is licensed rather than where it isn’t.

Your editorial call — keep this short

For mainland residents the article says one thing: this is illegal under Chinese law, full stop. No workaround, no caveats, no “but if you…”. One short paragraph, then move on. The detail below is background so you can write that paragraph accurately — most of it should not appear in the article.

The one distinction worth keeping in the prose is residence, because it decides which sentence a reader gets.

Case A — PRC passport, resident in HK / SG / elsewhere: workable

HashKey publishes an explicit “account opening using a Chinese passport” guide. Submit the PRC passport first (nationality: China, type: Passport), then submit a second overseas document establishing long-term residence — a non-mainland resident ID card, permanent resident visa, residence permit, work visa, student visa, or business visa.

  • Documents valid at least 3 months beyond the KYC submission date.
  • Original photographs only — no scans, no copies.
  • Passport data page fully unobstructed; no fingers or objects on the corners.
  • Initial deposit HKD 10,000 or USD equivalent — or complete verification in person at HashKey’s Hong Kong office.
Teaching point

A PRC passport alone is not sufficient. The second document does the work. Readers with HK or SG residence status already hold it and don’t realize it’s the key that unlocks this.

Case B — resident in mainland China: no clean answer

Post–Notice No. 42, offshore platforms are prohibited from serving mainland persons in any form. Aggregator sites still claim Kraken and OKX accept mainland ID — do not repeat that. It is SEO content, it may already be stale, and pointing friends toward it exposes them to a regime that treats this as illegal financial activity. Community reports also note that failed KYC attempts can flag a passport across multiple platforms, so casual experimentation carries a real cost.

Recommended framing

State the legal position, note that this is the one case where you can’t help, and stop there. Refusing to supply a workaround is the sovereignty-consistent position — and it protects you as a writer.

Why OKX isn’t the headline pick, despite the licences

A fair challenge to my earlier draft: I called OKX the most regulated of the large offshore venues, noted it is MAS-licensed, then quietly left it off the recommendation. That was inconsistent. Here is the actual reasoning, and the first draft’s omission was for the wrong reason.

The case, both sides
For

MAS Major Payment Institution licence since September 2024. A DBS partnership delivering free instant SGD deposits and withdrawals. PayNow at zero fee, FAST across 27 participating banks, Singpass onboarding in about a minute. On rails alone it matches Coinbase SG — better than I gave it credit for.

Against

In February 2025 OKX pleaded guilty to operating an unlicensed money-transmitting business and paid $505 million ($84m fine, $421m forfeiture). It processed over $1 trillion in US customer transactions while officially barring US users. DOJ found an employee coached US customers to lie about their residency, and that OKX ran no commercial AML monitoring software until roughly May 2023. It operates under an external compliance monitor through February 2027.

Verdict — and how much of it to publish

“Most regulated of the large offshore venues” was true but incomplete: that licence portfolio was assembled after, and partly because of, the plea. For an article telling non-crypto friends where to send their savings, a venue under an active DOJ compliance monitor shouldn’t be the headline pick — however good its PayNow integration is.

But you don’t need to publish the reasoning. “Coinbase is the most straightforward option for a first purchase in Singapore” is a positive recommendation that requires no comparison and takes no shot at anyone. The DOJ plea is public record and you have it if a reader asks directly — it just doesn’t need to be in the piece.

The Gracy Chen point — a genuinely useful lesson

Bitget’s CEO holds an NUS degree and is Singapore-connected, which makes the exchange feel Singaporean. It isn’t, in the only sense that matters here: where a company’s executives live has no bearing on where the company is licensed. Licensing follows the legal entity and the jurisdiction it serves, not the CEO’s address. That’s exactly the kind of reasonable-but-wrong proxy a non-crypto reader would rely on — worth one line in the article as a teaching moment.

How to handle it without a swipe

You don’t need to characterise Bitget at all. “Not currently available to Singapore residents — the platform blocks access itself” is accurate, sourced to Bitget’s own notice, and reads as a fact about availability rather than a judgment. A firm with pending EU authorisation and licences in El Salvador and Georgia is in transition, which is a fairer description than anything sharper.

Recommendation

Lead with Coinbase Singapore, name Crypto.com as the alternative, and mention Coinhako once as “the local incumbent if you want a Singapore-founded option.” OKX is legitimately available and worth one neutral line. Bitget only needs a mention if a reader is likely to ask — one sentence on availability. Drop Bitstamp and Independent Reserve entirely. Both are legitimate — Independent Reserve was in fact the first exchange MAS licensed — but they add names without adding value for a beginner, which is precisely the failure mode you want to avoid.

Retail restrictions readers must know before they try:

  • Locally issued credit cards cannot be used to buy crypto. Full stop.
  • A risk-awareness assessment is mandatory before retail access is granted — expect a quiz.
  • No leverage, margin, or financing for retail.
  • No airdrops, referral bonuses, or sign-up incentives — so if someone offers one, that says something about who you’re dealing with.
  • DPT services cannot be marketed to the Singapore public.
Opening hook

That last restriction is exactly why your Singapore friends have never seen a crypto ad — and why they end up asking you instead of Google. Good material for the intro.

The credit-card ban does not lock locals out

Worth stating plainly in the article, because it is a natural misreading: if locals hold locally issued credit cards and those cards can’t buy crypto, it can look as though MAS has quietly made crypto a foreigners-only product. It hasn’t.

01
The rule targets borrowed money, not local peopleIt bans buying a volatile asset on credit. It sits in the same rulebook as the bans on margin, leverage and financing for retail — one principle, three expressions.
02
Locals use PayNow and FAST insteadInstant transfers from their own SGD deposits, free, 24/7. A local with a DBS or OCBC account is fully served — and PayNow is more convenient than a card, not less.
03
The causation runs the other wayMAS could afford to close the credit rail because Singapore has world-class instant payment infrastructure. The ban costs locals almost nothing.
04
If anyone is inconvenienced, it’s foreignersA visitor without a local bank account can’t use PayNow and would otherwise have reached for a card. The restriction bites hardest on non-residents.
Transferable lesson worth a line in the article

When a regulation blocks one payment method, ask what the remaining methods are before concluding a class of people is excluded. Regulators usually close the risky rail and leave the safe one open.

Banking — the rail matters, not the bank

Your Capital One question, answered

Capital One blocks crypto purchases on its credit cards — volatility, fraud, and cash-advance exposure — but does not block ACH or bank transfers to licensed exchanges. Your experience is exactly right and matches published policy. It didn’t appear in the first draft because my source only covered the three largest retail banks; that was a sampling gap in my research, not evidence against Capital One.

The rule that generalizes, and it’s better than any bank-by-bank table: the question isn’t which bank — it’s which rail. Nearly every major US bank blocks crypto on credit cards while permitting ACH and wire to licensed exchanges. Tell readers to link a bank account and never a card. One sentence, no table to go stale — and card rails carry 3–4% fees anyway, so it’s also the cheaper advice.

The bigger 2026 shift: banks now sell Bitcoin directly

Per River Research (January 2026), 15 of the top 25 US banks — 60% — now offer or are building Bitcoin services spanning trading, custody, lending and advisory.

  • PNC — launched direct spot Bitcoin trading in December 2025 via Coinbase. PNC keeps the client relationship; Coinbase handles custody, execution and compliance behind the scenes.
  • JPMorgan Chase — partnership covering its 80m+ customers: Chase credit cards could fund Coinbase from autumn 2025, direct Chase-account linking to Coinbase during 2026, and Ultimate Rewards points redeemable for USDC.
  • Morgan Stanley — direct BTC, ETH and SOL trading on E*Trade in H1 2026, custody via Zerohash.
  • Wells Fargo — bitcoin-backed loans for institutional clients.

Note the irony worth flagging to readers

Even in the Chase–Coinbase deal, credit-card purchases may still be treated as cash advances. The “use a bank account, not a card” rule survives the partnership intact.

Where instinct is out of date

The Operation Chokepoint 2.0 era (2022–24) is over. The SEC rescinded SAB 121 in January 2025, removing the accounting rule that forced banks to book customer crypto as a balance-sheet liability. Silvergate and Signature both collapsed in March 2023 — that’s where most of the “banks hate crypto” folklore comes from. Transfers to Coinbase, Kraken and Gemini clear reliably; transfers to obscure venues do not.

This is your closer

The banks your friends already use are becoming Bitcoin on-ramps. That makes buying trivially easy — and makes the custody question more urgent, not less. Buying through Chase means Chase and Coinbase both know exactly what you hold. A clean bridge to self-custody, without preaching.

Editorial stance

Writing this without burning your friends

You work with people at these exchanges, and this piece isn’t an exposé. That constraint is easy to honour, because the article’s job and the exposé’s job are different questions:

Your article answers “where can I go?” It does not answer “where has everyone else been?” A reader deciding where to make a first Bitcoin purchase needs the first answer completely and doesn’t need the second at all. Most of the sharper material in this brief exists because you asked me analytical questions — it is background for your confidence, not copy for your readers.

01
Name where firms are licensed, not where they aren’t“OKX holds licences in the EU, Dubai and Singapore” carries the same decision-relevant information as “OKX is unlicensed in Hong Kong,” without reading as an accusation.
02
Let jurisdiction carry the structure“Different platforms are licensed in different places — here’s what’s licensed where you live” is a fact about regulation, not a judgment about any company. It also happens to be the most useful possible framing for the reader.
03
Recommend positively, don’t rank negatively“Coinbase is the most straightforward first purchase in Singapore” needs no comparison and takes no shot. Every venue you omit is omitted silently — you never have to say why.
04
Cut the exit timeline and the VPN channelThe withdrawal dates, the SFC alert listing, the DOJ plea and the VPN routing are all real and public — and none of them help a reader choose a venue. Keep them here as background.
05
Where a firm blocks a market itself, quote the firmBitget says Singapore is a Prohibited Country in its own terms. Sourcing an availability fact to the company’s own notice is the least contentious form it can take.
The one line that does the heavy lifting

“Different platforms are licensed in different places. Here’s what’s licensed where you live.” That sentence is reader-useful, factually unimpeachable, and would not annoy a single person you work with. Most of the article can hang off it.

One caveat worth being clear about

The restraint here is editorial fit, not suppression. The SFC alert list, the DOJ plea and MAS’s Investor Alert List are matters of public record, and you have them sourced if a reader asks you directly. You’re choosing not to lead with them because they don’t serve the reader’s decision — which is a much stronger reason than avoiding awkwardness, and it happens to produce the same article.

Before you publish

Editorial cautions

  • This brief is not the article. Roughly a third of what’s here — the exit timeline, the DOJ plea, the alert-list entries, the VPN channel — is background so you can write with confidence. Publishing it would turn a service piece into an exposé. See the editorial stance section above.
  • Regulatory lists rot fast. The SFC list gained two entries in the last six months alone. Date-stamp the article explicitly — “as of August 2026” — and link to the primary registers so the piece degrades gracefully instead of becoming wrong.
  • Link regulators, not review sites. Nearly every “top 10 exchanges 2026” result I hit was affiliate SEO content with inconsistent numbers.
  • Volume rankings are noise here. Binance ran ~38.7% of top-10 CEX spot volume in Q2 2026, Bybit ~10%, Coinbase ~8.6% in Q1 — none of which helps a Hong Kong reader who legally can’t use two of the three. Leave rankings out; including them invites exactly the wrong mental model.
  • Don’t publish a fee table. Fees change monthly, and a stale table is worse than none.
  • Two name corrections from your outline. Huobi is now HTX; “BGET” is Bitget — and note that Bitget is distinct from BGE, the SFC-licensed Hong Kong entity. A genuinely confusable pair, worth a parenthetical.
  • Deliberately excluded as unverified: per-bank block and allow behavior in HK and SG beyond documented HSBC policy, and whether individual HK banks silently flag exchange transfers. Only community anecdote exists, so per your call it stays out.
