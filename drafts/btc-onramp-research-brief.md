# Research Brief — "Where Can I Buy Bitcoin?"
**Prepared 2026-08-25 · for Digital Sovereignty Chronicle · verifiable claims only**

---

## 0. Three findings that change the article

**1. Your HSBC premise doesn't hold in Hong Kong.**
HSBC blocks or throttles crypto in the UK (£2,500/txn, £10,000 per 30 days) and blocks
outright in Australia. Credit-card crypto purchases are blocked globally. But **Hong Kong is
HSBC's most permissive market** — HSBC HK does not block transfers to SFC-licensed platforms,
has offered crypto ETF access via its HK brokerage since mid-2023, and in April 2026 received
one of the HKMA's first two **stablecoin issuer licences**. The "HSBC is bad for crypto"
reputation is real, but it's a *UK/Australia* story that HK readers have absorbed by osmosis.
Correcting this is a genuinely useful thing your article can do. (If you have first-hand
accounts of HK HSBC blocks, that's worth a line — but it's anecdote against a documented
policy, so frame it as such.)

**2. The real bank problem in HK is the reverse of what you'd expect.**
The friction isn't "which bank lets you send money out." It's that the licensed exchanges
require the first deposit to come from **a bank account in your own name** (HKD 10,000 / USD
equivalent minimum at both HashKey and OSL). No third-party accounts, no joint accounts. The
gating factor is *having the right kind of bank account*, not which bank it is.

**3. The mainland-passport answer changed in February 2026 — this is the big one.**
On **6 Feb 2026**, eight PRC regulators (PBOC, NDRC, MIIT, NFRA, CSRC, SAFE, et al.) issued the
*Notice on Further Preventing and Dealing with Virtual Currency and Other Related Risks*
(Notice No. 42 [2026]). It extends the 2021 ban: **offshore entities and individuals are now
prohibited from providing virtual-currency services to persons in mainland China in any form.**
Previously the restriction bit at onshore employees/affiliates; now it reaches the offshore
provider itself.

This means the honest answer to a mainland-resident friend is no longer "use OKX with your
passport." **The dividing line for your whole audience is not nationality — it's residence.**
A PRC passport holder *residing in* Hong Kong or Singapore has good, legal options. A PRC
passport holder *residing in* the mainland does not have a clean answer, and you should not
give them a workaround. Say so plainly and stop there.

## 1. The short answer

For someone with no crypto background, the answer is a **licensed centralized exchange in the
jurisdiction where they legally reside**. Not the biggest exchange, not the one with the best
fees — the one that will actually accept their ID, take their money from a local bank, and
still be there next year.

Everything below is organized by residence, because residence is what determines the answer.

### Mainland China passport / mainland residence

**Split this into two cases — your readers will conflate them and the difference is everything.**

**Case A — PRC passport, resident in HK/SG/elsewhere: workable.**
HashKey publishes an explicit *"account opening using a Chinese passport"* guide. The process:
submit the PRC passport (nationality: China, type: Passport), **then submit a second overseas
document** establishing long-term residence — a non-mainland resident ID card, permanent
resident visa, residence permit, work visa, student visa, or business visa. Documents must be
valid ≥3 months out, originals only (no scans or copies), passport data page fully unobstructed.
Initial deposit HKD 10,000 / USD equivalent, or complete verification in person at HashKey's
Hong Kong office.

The teaching point: **a PRC passport alone is not sufficient.** The second document is what
does the work. Your readers with HK/SG residence status already have it and don't realize it's
the key that unlocks this.

**Case B — resident in mainland China: no clean answer, and you should say so.**
Post-Notice No. 42, offshore platforms are prohibited from serving mainland persons in any
form. Aggregator sites still claim Kraken and OKX accept mainland ID — **do not repeat that**.
It's SEO content, it may already be stale, and pointing friends toward it exposes them to a
regime that treats this as illegal financial activity. Community reports also note that failed
KYC attempts can flag a passport across multiple platforms, so casual experimentation carries
a real cost.

Recommended framing: state the legal position, note that this is the one case where you can't
help, and leave it there. Refusing to give a workaround *is* the sovereignty-consistent
position — and it protects you as a writer.

### Singapore

**Regulator:** MAS, under the Payment Services Act 2019 (amended 2024). ~36–37 firms hold Major
Payment Institution licences covering DPT services as of Jan 2026 — but that list mixes
exchanges, custodians, payment apps (PayPal, Revolut), brokers (moomoo, DBS Vickers) and
stablecoin issuers (StraitsX, Circle). It is *not* a list of 37 places to buy Bitcoin.

**Practical retail venues:** Coinbase Singapore (MPI licence, Singpass onboarding, free instant
SGD via PayNow and FAST, customer funds under statutory trust), Coinhako (local), Independent
Reserve, Crypto.com, Bitstamp, OKX (DPT-licensed in SG specifically).

**Retail restrictions your readers must know before they try:**
- **Locally issued credit cards cannot be used to buy crypto.** Full stop.
- A **risk-awareness assessment is mandatory** before retail access is granted — expect a quiz.
- No leverage, margin, or financing for retail.
- No airdrops, referral bonuses, or sign-up incentives (so if someone offers you one, that's a
  signal about who you're dealing with).
- DPT services can't be marketed to the Singapore public — which is exactly why your friends
  have never seen an ad and end up asking you instead. Nice detail for the intro.

**Funding:** PayNow / FAST are the rails. Free and instant on Coinbase SG.

### United States

**Practical retail venues:** Coinbase, Kraken, Gemini, Crypto.com, Binance.US.

**Availability notes:** Binance.US is unavailable in **New York, Alaska, Washington, and
Georgia**, and offers spot only (no futures/margin). **Bybit and OKX's global platform do not
serve US residents.** Binance's global platform does not serve US residents.

**Banking:** This is the section where your instinct is most out of date. Chase, Bank of
America and Wells Fargo all permit ACH and wire to licensed exchanges. ACH is free at Coinbase,
Kraken and Gemini; wires run ~$25 at Gemini and Coinbase. The Operation Chokepoint 2.0 era
(2022–24) ended: SEC rescinded SAB 121 in January 2025, removing the accounting rule that made
banks treat customer crypto as a balance-sheet liability. Silvergate and Signature both
collapsed in March 2023, which is where most of the "banks hate crypto" folklore comes from.
Transfers to Coinbase/Kraken/Gemini clear reliably; transfers to obscure venues do not.

### Canada

**Regulator:** CSA, via the Crypto Asset Trading Platform registration regime. (I could not
fetch the CSA's authorized-platform list directly — it 307-redirects and blocks automated
fetches. **Verify the current list at securities-administrators.ca before publishing.**)

**Confirmed registered:** Coinbase (Restricted Dealer, April 2024 — first international
exchange registered in Canada), Kraken / Payward Canada Inc. (CATP decision 1 Apr 2025),
Shakepay (FINTRAC + CSA + OSC; CATP decision 6 Jul 2026).

**Confirmed absent:** **Binance withdrew from Canada in May 2023**, citing CSA requirements on
stablecoins and derivatives as making operation "no longer tenable." Bybit restricts Canada.

**Funding:** Interac e-Transfer is the native rail — free on Coinbase, $2–$10,000 CAD per
transaction, usually minutes. Also EFT (free, 1–3 business days), PayPal ($1,000 CAD/day),
debit/credit card (3.99% — tell readers to avoid).

## 3. Suggested structure

Following your Proton BTC piece: short, numbered, practical, one clear opinion at the end.

1. **The question I keep getting** — friends from TradFi, market's up, "where do I buy?"
   Hook: in Singapore they've never seen a crypto ad because MAS bans marketing it. That's
   why they're asking you and not Google.
2. **The short answer** — a licensed exchange where you legally live. Then the reframe:
   *the question isn't which exchange is best, it's which one will take you.*
3. **It's residence, not passport** — the single idea that organizes everything. Set this up
   early; it's the load-bearing insight and it's what your audience gets wrong.
4. **Hong Kong** — the 13-name SFC list, then the punchline that none of the famous names are
   on it. OSL/HashKey. ZA Bank as settlement bank. The HKD 10,000 own-name first deposit.
5. **Singapore** — MAS, the credit-card ban, the mandatory risk quiz, PayNow.
6. **US / Canada** — brief; Coinbase/Kraken/Gemini, Binance.US state gaps, Binance's Canadian
   exit, Interac.
7. **The mainland question** — handle honestly. Case A (passport + overseas residence) works
   and here's how. Case B (mainland residence) doesn't, post-Feb-2026, and you're not going to
   pretend otherwise.
8. **About banks (the HSBC myth)** — correct the record. HSBC HK ≠ HSBC UK. The real friction
   is own-name accounts and first-deposit minimums.
9. **First three steps** — pick the licensed venue for where you live; verify with the
   regulator's own list, not a review site; fund from your own bank account.
10. **The closer** — natural bridge to your existing work: an exchange is a custodian you rent.
    Buying is step one; getting the coins off the exchange is step two. Link the Proton BTC
    piece and *How To Protect Your Crypto Assets*.

**Length:** 900–1,300 words. Your Proton piece was 472 and worked because it was one task.
This has five jurisdictions, so it needs more room — but resist going past ~1,300 or it stops
being the bookmarkable reference your friends actually want.

## 4. Editorial cautions

- **Regulatory lists rot fast.** The SFC list gained two entries in the last six months alone.
  Date-stamp the article explicitly ("as of August 2026") and link readers to the primary
  registers so the piece degrades gracefully instead of becoming wrong.
- **Link regulators, not review sites.** SFC VATP register, MAS Financial Institutions
  Directory (eservices.mas.gov.sg/fid), CSA authorized platforms. Nearly every "top 10
  exchanges 2026" result I hit was affiliate SEO content with inconsistent numbers.
- **Volume rankings are noise for this article.** Binance ~38.7% of top-10 CEX spot volume in
  Q2 2026, Bybit ~10%, Coinbase ~8.6% in Q1 — none of which helps a HK reader who legally
  can't use two of the three. I'd leave rankings out entirely; including them invites exactly
  the wrong mental model.
- **Don't publish a fee table.** Fees change monthly and a stale table is worse than none.
- **Two name corrections from your outline:** Huobi is now **HTX**; "BGET" is **Bitget** — and
  note Bitget is distinct from **BGE**, the SFC-licensed HK entity, which is a genuinely
  confusable pair worth a parenthetical.
- **Unverified, deliberately excluded:** per-bank block/allow behavior in HK and SG beyond the
  documented HSBC policy; whether individual HK banks silently flag exchange transfers. Only
  community anecdote exists, so per your call it's out.

## US banking — the section to expand (this is now a better story)

**Your Capital One question, answered:** Capital One blocks **credit card** crypto purchases —
volatility/fraud/cash-advance risk. It does **not** block bank transfers or ACH to exchanges.
Your experience is exactly right and matches policy. It didn't surface earlier because my
source only covered the three largest retail banks; that was a sampling gap, not evidence.

**The rule that actually generalizes — and this is the useful reframe for readers:**
The question is not *which bank*. It's *which rail*. Nearly every major US bank blocks crypto
purchases on **credit cards** (cash-advance and fraud exposure) while permitting **ACH and
wire** to licensed exchanges. Tell readers to link a bank account, never a credit card. That
one sentence replaces any bank-by-bank table — and it's also cheaper, since card rails carry
3–4% fees anyway.

**The bigger 2026 development — banks now sell Bitcoin directly:**
Per River Research (Jan 2026), **15 of the top 25 US banks (60%)** now offer or are building
Bitcoin services — trading, custody, lending, advisory.

- **PNC** — launched direct spot Bitcoin trading Dec 2025 via Coinbase; PNC holds the client
  relationship, Coinbase does custody/execution/compliance behind the scenes.
- **JPMorgan Chase** — partnership with Coinbase covering its 80m+ customers: Chase credit
  cards could fund Coinbase from fall 2025; **direct Chase-account linking to Coinbase in
  2026**; Ultimate Rewards points redeemable for USDC. (Note the irony for your readers: the
  credit-card route here may still carry cash-advance terms.)
- **Morgan Stanley** — direct BTC/ETH/SOL trading on E*Trade, H1 2026, custody via Zerohash.
- **Wells Fargo** — bitcoin-backed loans for institutional clients.
- **Citi** — exploring custody; more focused on tokenizing infrastructure than retail trading.
- **PNC + Citi + Wells Fargo** — exploring a joint stablecoin via Early Warning Services
  (Zelle's parent).

**Why this matters to your thesis:** this is a genuinely good hook for the closer. The banks
your friends already use are becoming crypto on-ramps — which makes buying trivially easy and
makes the custody question *more* urgent, not less. Buying Bitcoin through Chase means Chase
and Coinbase both know. That's a clean bridge to self-custody without being preachy.
